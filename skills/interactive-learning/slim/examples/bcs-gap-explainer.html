Why does the BCS gap open like a square root?

The critical temperature depends on the coupling in a wildly non-analytic way. The gap below it does not. One picture of the gap equation shows why.

Weak-coupling BCS: constant density of states \(N(0)\), attraction for \(|\xi| \lt \omega_D\), \(\lambda = N(0)V = 0.3\); \(k_B = \hbar = 1\). Energies in units of \(T_c\), where the results do not depend on \(\lambda\).

The BCS critical temperature, \(T_c \approx 1.13\,\omega_D\,e^{-1/\lambda}\), is not even analytic in the coupling: no power series in \(\lambda\) reaches it. So how does the gap \(\Delta(T)\) appear as the metal is cooled through \(T_c\)?

Dividing the BCS gap equation by \(\Delta\) leaves a condition on a single function of \(\Delta\) and \(T\):

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} 1 &= I(\Delta, T), \\ I(\Delta, T) &= \lambda \int_0^{\omega_D} \! \dd\xi\, \frac{\tanh(E/2T)}{E}, \end{aligned} \label{eq:gap} \end{equation}

with quasiparticle energy \(E = \sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta^2}\). The equilibrium gap is where the curve \(I(\Delta, T)\) crosses the level 1. A gap raises every \(E\), and \(\tanh(E/2T)/E\) falls with \(E\), so \(I\) is largest at \(\Delta = 0\). As \(T\) falls, \(I(0, T)\) grows and passes 1 exactly at \(T_c\).

  • Start at \(T = 1.2\,T_c\): the curve stays below 1 everywhere, so only \(\Delta = 0\) solves the gap equation.
  • Lower \(T\) slowly through \(T_c\) and watch the top of the curve near \(\Delta = 0\), and how far out it crosses 1.
  • Go to low \(T\) and compare the curve with the dashed parabola.

Where the square root comes from

Near \(T_c\) only the top of the curve matters, and the top is a parabola. \(I\) depends on \(\Delta\) only through \(\Delta^2\) and is smooth there for \(T \gt 0\), so to leading order

\begin{equation} I(\Delta, T) \approx I(0, T) - \frac{7\zeta(3)\,\lambda}{8\pi^2}\,\frac{\Delta^2}{T^2} . \label{eq:top} \end{equation}

Meanwhile the height of the top above 1 grows linearly: \(I(0, T) - 1 = \lambda \ln(T_c/T) \approx \lambda\,(1 - T/T_c)\). A parabola raised by \(\varepsilon\) crosses the level at \(\Delta \propto \sqrt{\varepsilon}\). Setting \eqref{eq:top} equal to 1:

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \Delta^2 &\approx \frac{8\pi^2}{7\zeta(3)}\, T_c^2 \Big(1 - \frac{T}{T_c}\Big), \\ \Delta &\approx 3.06\, T_c \sqrt{1 - T/T_c} . \end{aligned} \label{eq:sqrt} \end{equation}

The coupling \(\lambda\) cancels. Its essential singularity sits entirely in the scale \(T_c\); measured in units of \(T_c\), the gap equation near \(T_c\) is a smooth function of \(\Delta^2\) and \(T\), and \(\Delta^2\) comes out linear in \(T_c - T\). Switch the gap plot to \((\Delta/T_c)^2\) to see the straight line. This is the mean-field exponent \(\beta = 1/2\), the same mechanism as in Landau theory. At low \(T\) the expansion \eqref{eq:top} fails (the dashed parabola leaves the curve): at \(T = 0\), \(I\) has a \(\ln\Delta\) term instead of a \(\Delta^2\) term.
