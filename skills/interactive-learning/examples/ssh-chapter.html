The SSH chain: end states from a bulk winding number

A chain of identical atoms with alternating bonds has two insulating phases with the same bands. Only one of them has states at its ends. This chapter shows why, and what protects them.

Conventions: lattice constant 1; spinless electrons, one orbital per site, on-site energies 0; hoppings \(v, w \ge 0\); energies in the units of \(v, w\) Reading time about 20 minutes

An end that should not be special

Take a long chain of identical atoms, one orbital per atom, and let an electron hop between nearest neighbours. With equal bonds you get a single cosine band, and nothing interesting happens at the ends.

Now let the bonds alternate: strong, weak, strong, weak. Every atom is still the same, with the same on-site energy. The alternation opens a gap in the middle of the band. Then cut the chain.

The model

This is the Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model. It was introduced for polyacetylene and is now the standard first example of a topological band structure. Group the atoms into unit cells of two sites, A and B. An electron hops inside a cell with amplitude \(v\) and between neighbouring cells with amplitude \(w\):

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} H &= v \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big( c^\dagger_{n,A} c_{n,B} + \text{h.c.} \big) \\ &+ w \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \big( c^\dagger_{n+1,A} c_{n,B} + \text{h.c.} \big). \end{aligned} \label{eq:H} \end{equation}

The chain has \(N\) cells and \(2N\) sites. It starts with site \(A_1\) and ends with site \(B_N\), so both outermost bonds are intracell \(v\) bonds. When only the ratio matters, we set \(w = 1\). The model has no on-site energies at all: whatever happens at the ends cannot come from the end atoms being different.

By the end you can
  • compute the bulk bands and the gap of the SSH chain;
  • compute its winding number and predict from it, without diagonalising anything, whether an open chain has zero-energy end states;
  • derive how far such a state reaches into the chain;
  • name the symmetry that protects it, and say what destroys it.

Three questions should now be on your mind. Why do the end states sit at exactly zero energy, not just somewhere in the gap? Why do they exist for \(v \lt w\) but not for \(v \gt w\), when the ends look the same? And why do they spread out as \(v \to w\)? The rest of the chapter answers them, starting from the bulk.

The bulk: bands and a gap

In an infinite chain, translation by one cell is a symmetry. The Fourier transform \(c_{n,\alpha} = N^{-1/2} \sum_k e^{ikn} c_{k,\alpha}\) turns \eqref{eq:H} into independent \(2\times2\) blocks, one per wavenumber \(k\), with the Bloch Hamiltonian

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} h(k) &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & v + w e^{-ik} \\ v + w e^{ik} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ &= d_x(k)\,\sigma_x + d_y(k)\,\sigma_y , \end{aligned} \label{eq:hk} \end{equation}

in the basis \((A, B)\). The vector \(\mathbf d(k)\) and the band energies are

\begin{align} \mathbf d(k) &= \big(v + w\cos k,\ w \sin k,\ 0\big), \label{eq:d} \\ E_\pm(k) &= \pm|\mathbf d(k)| \nonumber \\ &= \pm\sqrt{v^2 + w^2 + 2vw\cos k}. \label{eq:E} \end{align}

The two terms of \(v + w e^{ik}\) partly cancel at the zone boundary \(k = \pi\), so the gap is \(2|v - w|\). It closes only at \(v = w\), where the chain is uniform and the spectrum is the gapless cosine band folded into the smaller zone.

What the bands do not show: winding

The energies forget something that the eigenstates remember. Because \(h(k)\) has no \(\sigma_z\) term, \(\mathbf d(k)\) lies in the \(d_x\)–\(d_y\) plane, and the eigenstates are fixed by the angle \(\varphi(k)\) of \(\mathbf d(k)\) in that plane: the lower band is \(\ket{u_-(k)} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,(e^{-i\varphi(k)}, -1)^T\).

As \(k\) runs once through the Brillouin zone, \(\mathbf d(k)\) traces a closed loop. By \eqref{eq:d} it is a circle of radius \(w\) centred at \((v, 0)\). The winding number \(W\) counts how many times this loop goes around the origin, which is the same as the total change of \(\varphi\) divided by \(2\pi\).

So the bulk carries an integer that tells \((v, w)\) apart from \((w, v)\) and can change only where the gap closes. It also matches what the open chain did: \(W = 1\) with end states, \(W = 0\) without. The section The general statement makes this connection precise. First, a closer look at the end states.

Where the end states live

For a finite chain, the two states nearest zero are, up to an exponentially small mixing, even and odd combinations of a left end state and a right end state. Two things are visible. Near the left end the weight sits only on A sites, near the right end only on B sites. And on the log plot the computed points fall on a straight line, so the decay is exponential, with a length that grows as \(v \to w\). Close to \(v = w\) the points leave the line at the far end: the state now reaches the other end of the chain and mixes with the right end state.

The general statement

Chiral symmetry

Every term of \eqref{eq:H} moves an electron from A to B or back. Let \(\Gamma = \sum_n \big(\ket{n,A}\!\bra{n,A} - \ket{n,B}\!\bra{n,B}\big)\) be the sublattice operator, \(\sigma_z\) in every cell. Then

\begin{equation} \Gamma H \Gamma = -H, \qquad \Gamma^2 = 1 . \label{eq:chiral} \end{equation}

  1. If \(H\psi = E\psi\), then \(H(\Gamma\psi) = -E\,\Gamma\psi\): the spectrum is symmetric about zero. The flow plot in the first widget is mirror symmetric.
  2. The zero-energy eigenspace is mapped to itself by \(\Gamma\), so zero modes can be chosen to live on one sublattice only.
  3. A state on one sublattice is its own chiral partner (\(\Gamma\psi = \pm\psi\)). It cannot move away from \(E = 0\) on its own: that needs a partner at \(-E\), so it must mix with a state on the other sublattice.

In \(k\)-space, \eqref{eq:chiral} reads \(\sigma_z h(k) \sigma_z = -h(k)\), which forces \(d_z = 0\). That is why \(\mathbf d(k)\) lies in a plane and why its winding around the origin is defined at all.

The winding number

Write \(q(k) = d_x + i d_y\). The winding number is the number of turns of \(q(k)\) around zero:

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} W &= \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_{-\pi}^{\pi} \dd k\ \partial_k \ln q(k), \\ q(k) &= v + w\, e^{ik}. \end{aligned} \label{eq:W} \end{equation}

With \(z = e^{ik}\) the integral runs around the unit circle, and by the argument principle it counts the zeros of \(v + wz\) inside it. The only zero, \(z = -v/w\), is inside if and only if \(v \lt w\). Hence \(W = 1\) for \(v \lt w\) and \(W = 0\) for \(v \gt w\). At \(v = w\) the zero sits on the circle, \(q(\pi) = 0\), and \(W\) is undefined: the gap is closed.

Bulk–boundary correspondence

Statement Take a chain with chiral symmetry \eqref{eq:chiral}, short-range hopping and a gapped bulk with winding number \(W\). At an edge far from the other edge, the number of zero-energy states on sublattice A minus the number on sublattice B equals \(W\) (the sign convention flips at the opposite end).

The argument fits in three steps. By point 3 above, zero modes at one edge can leave \(E = 0\) only in A–B pairs, so \(N_A - N_B\) cannot change under any deformation that keeps chiral symmetry and a bulk gap. It therefore depends only on the bulk phase. Finally, evaluate it in the two dimer limits: at \(v = 0\), site \(A_1\) is isolated (\(N_A - N_B = 1 = W\)); at \(w = 0\), every site is paired (\(0 = W\)).

The zero mode and its length

On a semi-infinite chain \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), the zero mode is exact. Its amplitudes are

\begin{equation} \psi_{n,A} = \Big(-\frac{v}{w}\Big)^{n-1} \psi_{1,A}, \qquad \psi_{n,B} = 0, \label{eq:zm} \end{equation}

so its density decays as

\begin{equation} |\psi_{n,A}|^2 \propto e^{-2(n-1)/\xi}, \qquad \xi = \frac{1}{\ln(w/v)} . \label{eq:xi} \end{equation}

Derivation of \eqref{eq:zm}

Write \(\psi = \sum_n \big(a_n \ket{n,A} + b_n \ket{n,B}\big)\) and project \(H\psi = 0\) onto each site. On \(B_n\): \(v\,a_n + w\,a_{n+1} = 0\). On \(A_n\): \(v\,b_n + w\,b_{n-1} = 0\), with \(b_0 = 0\) because there is no site \(B_0\).

The second equation at \(n = 1\) gives \(b_1 = 0\), and then \(b_n = 0\) for all \(n\). The first gives \(a_{n+1} = -(v/w)\,a_n\), which is \eqref{eq:zm}. The norm \(\sum_n |a_n|^2 = |a_1|^2 / (1 - v^2/w^2)\) is finite only for \(v \lt w\), so \(a_1 = \sqrt{1 - v^2/w^2}\). For \(v \gt w\) there is no normalisable zero mode at the left end.

This exact envelope is the dashed line in the first widget and in the end-state widget.

The mode is normalisable only for \(v \lt w\), which is exactly the condition \(W = 1\) found from the bulk. Near the transition \(\xi \approx w/(w - v)\) diverges, as it must where the gap closes. In a chain of \(N\) cells the two end modes overlap by about \(e^{-N/\xi} = (v/w)^N\). That overlap splits their energies to \(\pm\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \propto (v/w)^N\): the tiny number in the readout of the first widget.

Back to the opening question

It depends on where the chain is cut. Our chain ends with an intracell \(v\) bond at both ends. If that end bond is the weak one (\(v \lt w\)), each end carries one state at exactly \(E = 0\), in the middle of the gap. If it is the strong one (\(v \gt w\)), there is none. Nothing about the end atoms differs between the two cases: same atoms, same missing bond, opposite result.

The reason is in the bulk. The two phases have identical bands, but their eigenstates wind differently: \(W = 1\) or \(0\), and \(W\) can change only where the gap closes. Chiral symmetry makes zero modes sublattice-polarised and holds them at \(E = 0\), so the count of zero modes at an edge is protected and must equal \(W\).

“No, the atoms are all identical” is a reasonable answer: it is correct about the atoms and wrong about what decides the question, which is the topology of the bulk eigenstates. “Any end binds a state” fails too: the \(v \gt w\) chain has ends and no end states. Ordinary surface states do occur in many systems, but they need something at the surface to bind them, and their energy is not pinned to the middle of the gap.

Summary

  • The SSH chain has two gapped phases with identical bulk bands \(E_\pm = \pm|v + w e^{ik}|\). They differ by the winding number of \(\mathbf d(k)\) around the origin: \(W = 1\) for \(v \lt w\), \(W = 0\) for \(v \gt w\).
  • \(W\) is defined because chiral symmetry keeps \(d_z = 0\). It can change only where the gap closes; a gap closing is necessary for a change of \(W\), not sufficient.
  • Bulk–boundary correspondence: at an edge, (zero modes on A) − (zero modes on B) \(= W\). For SSH this is one end state per edge at \(E = 0\), on one sublattice, decaying over \(\xi = 1/\ln(w/v)\).
  • Pitfall: the protection relies on chiral symmetry. A staggered potential or hopping within one sublattice moves the end states away from zero, although they may stay inside the gap for a while.
